---
title: "Per-request limits are not enough"
description: "₹9,999 twenty times is still ₹2 lakh. Why agents need limits over time, why who keeps the count matters most, and the design for cumulative grant limits (not built yet)."
---

Give your refund agent a sensible rule: **no single refund above ₹10,000**. Enforce it properly, outside the agent, in
code the agent cannot touch.

Now a scripted abuse campaign hits your support chat: fifty accounts, each asking for a ₹9,999 refund. Every request is
within the limit. By the time anyone looks, about ₹5 lakh is gone, and every single decision was "correct". (An
illustration, not an incident.)

Per-request limits stop **one big mistake**. They do nothing about **many small ones**: a loop, a bug, an attacker who
has read your limit, or an agent that has decided refunding everyone is the fastest way to close tickets.

The next control is a **cumulative limit**. Parmana's grants have per-request limits today; cumulative limits are
**designed, not built**. This page is the design, and a lesson from a version that was built wrong.

## What a cumulative limit says

> This agent may refund at most ₹50,000, or 20 refunds, per day, in Indian time.

It sounds like a counter. It is harder than that, for three reasons.

## Problem 1: who is counting?

The tempting shortcut is to let the caller tell you the running total: "this refund brings today's total to ₹42,000".

An early payment connector in Parmana did exactly this: it took the day's cumulative total from the caller. Parmana's
own certification named it an exception to the core promise that an AI cannot execute what the business has not
authorized. If the caller says the total is ₹0, the limit is decoration. It was replaced with a server-side ledger that
Parmana counts itself, and that connector was later removed, but the lesson is now a rule of the design:

**The running total belongs to the authorization layer, counted from its own decisions. It is never declared by the
agent or read from the request.**

## Problem 2: two requests at the same moment

Two refunds of ₹6,000 arrive in the same millisecond; today's total is ₹40,000. Each reads ₹40,000, adds ₹6,000, gets
₹46,000 and passes. Together they reach ₹52,000.

The fix is to never "read, then check, then write". Reserve in **one atomic step**: add the amount only if the new
total stays within the cap. If two requests race, exactly one wins. The earlier server-side ledger did this and was
proven with 50 concurrent reservations; the design generalizes it from one connector to every grant.

## Problem 3: what counts as spent?

The amount is reserved when the decision is made, but not every decision ends with money moving:

- **Refused later in the same decision** (business validation fails, or the approval is missing): the reservation is
  released.
- **Refused at release** (the grant was revoked, or a fact expired): released.
- **Outcome unknown** (the call failed, so whether it ran is unknown): it keeps counting until a person resolves it.
  If money might have moved, assume it did.

And the total belongs to the **agent and capability, not the grant**. Otherwise revoking a grant and granting it again
would reset the day's count.

## What the record shows

Each decision would record, signed, the period, the total before and after, and the cap: "₹42,000 before, ₹48,000
after, cap ₹50,000". If the counter cannot be read at all, the answer is `AUTHORITY_UNCLEAR`, and the request is
refused. A limit that switches off when its storage fails is not a limit.

## Open questions

1. **Calendar day or rolling 24 hours?** Calendar periods match how businesses state limits; rolling windows are harder
   to game at midnight but cost more.
2. **Can one limit span several capabilities**, for example all refunds across all gateways?
3. **Can a signed human approval exceed the limit?** The default proposed is no, matching today's per-request grant
   check, which an approval cannot override.
4. **Where does the total live without a database?** An in-memory counter restarts at zero.

## What exists today

Grants with **per-request** limits (`min`, `max`, `oneOf`) and an end date are built, deployed and passed a live check
in production. **Cumulative limits are not built.** The full design, with the tests to write, is in RFC-0023, section
"Cumulative grant limits".

## The takeaway

Ask three questions about any agent's limits: is there a limit over time, not just per action? Who keeps the running
total? Does it hold when two requests race, when a call times out, and when the counter is unavailable?
