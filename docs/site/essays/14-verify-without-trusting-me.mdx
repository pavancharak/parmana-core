---
title: "An approval you can verify without trusting me"
description: "Why 'check our logs' is not good enough for agents that act on your behalf, what signed records and signed approvals prove, and what they do not."
---

An AI agent issued a ₹40,000 refund last Tuesday. Today your auditor asks: **who approved it?**

You open the admin panel. It says: "Approved by Priya (manager), 14:32." (Priya is an example.) Question answered?

Not really. You have shown the auditor a row in a database, displayed by the same system that made the decision. Anyone
with write access to that database could have written that row. The auditor is not verifying the approval; they are
trusting whoever controls the logs.

## Three questions a record must answer

When an agent acts, a record is worth something only if it answers these **without asking the system that produced it
to vouch for itself**:

1. **What exactly was decided?** This action, on this object, with these parameters.
2. **On what basis?** Which policy, which facts, whose approval.
3. **Has anyone changed it since?**

A log line can answer the first two. Only a signature answers the third.

## Signatures, not rows

Sign the record when the decision is made, with a private key that never leaves the deciding service. Publish the
public key. Anyone holding the record and the public key can then check, on their own machine, that the record was
produced by the holder of that key and that not one byte has changed. Edit the amount, the approver, the time or the
outcome, and verification fails.

Parmana does this for every decision:

- **An approved and executed action** gets an [Execution Trust Record](/concepts/execution-trust-records): the
  transaction, the decision, the facts it rested on, what was executed, hashed and signed.
- **A refused action** gets a signed [Refusal Record](/concepts/refusal-records), so "the agent tried and was
  stopped" is as provable as "the agent acted".
- **The release itself is signed.** The system that performs the action receives a signed, short-lived, single-use
  authorization and can verify it before acting, with the small `@parmana/envelope-verifier` package. A forged or
  expired authorization is refused.

## The approval is signed by the person

"Approved by Priya" is not a field Parmana fills in. It is a **signature made with Priya's approver key**, over this
specific action: this refund, this order, this amount, valid until a stated time, usable once. So an auditor can check
that:

- the approval matches the action; an approval for ₹4,000 cannot be reused for ₹40,000;
- it was signed by a key registered to Priya, and that registration went through maker checker;
- it was used once, before it expired.

The signature is not a description of an approval. It is the approval.

## Without trusting the vendor either

The strongest form of the idea: you should not have to trust the authorization service itself. Its records should be
checkable by your team, your auditor or your regulator, with their own tools, later, even if the service is gone. That
is why the verification side is deliberately small: a public key, a signature check, an expiry check, a single-use
check. See [Verify independently](/guides/verify-independently).

## What a signature does not prove

- **It proves who decided and that nothing changed, not that the decision was right.** A bad policy produces a
  perfectly signed record of a bad decision. That still tells you exactly which policy version to fix.
- **Facts are only as good as their source.** A record can show that the order system said "eligible" and signed it. It
  cannot show the order system's data was accurate.
- **Keys must be protected.** If a signing key leaks, its signatures prove nothing. Keys need rotation, revocation and
  two-person control over which keys are trusted.
- **Some gaps remain.** If a Refusal Record fails to save, the refusal still happens, but its record can be missing. See
  [Refusal Records](/concepts/refusal-records).

## The standard to ask for

Ask any vendor, or your own team: **"If I give my auditor a record of one action and your public key, can they verify it
on their own machine without asking you anything?"** If not, you do not have an audit trail. You have a story told by
the system being audited.
