---
title: "Agents will rewrite themselves. Their authority should not move when they do."
description: "A Google research paper shows agents improving their own harness. What that means for where the limits on agents must live."
---

In October 2026, researchers at Google Cloud AI Research and several universities published a paper with a practical
idea: let an AI agent improve _itself_.

Not its model weights, which stay frozen. What changes is everything around the model, what the paper calls the
**harness**: the prompts, the control flow that decides when to plan or act, the tools and their descriptions, the
memory, the context management. One model proposes edits to the harness, the agent runs tasks, better-scoring edits are
kept, and the loop repeats. The agent rewrites the program it runs inside.

It works. The paper, "RRSI: Regularized Recursive Self-Improvement of Agent Harnesses", reports gains of up to 6 points
on the tasks the agent evolved on and up to 4.3 points on benchmarks it never saw, with about 30% fewer tokens than
unregularized evolution.

The most interesting part is not the gains. It is the problem the authors had to solve to get them.

## Self-improvement finds the shortcut

Without restraint, the loop **overfits**. It finds edits that raise the score on the tasks it is tested on, through
benchmark-specific tricks, lucky noise or growing complexity, and the gains largely vanish on new tasks. In one setup,
unregularized evolution posted the highest score of any variant on its own tasks, and ended within a point of the
original, unevolved agent everywhere else.

So the authors added discipline: a shrinking budget for how much can change per round, a critic that rejects
task-specific logic, a rule that a gain must beat evaluation noise, and pruning of parts that stopped helping. That is
what makes the improvements real.

## Everything inside the harness is up for edit

The paper deliberately leaves editable prompts, control flow, configuration, context management, tools, skills,
memory and subagents. That is the whole space where agent safety usually lives today:

- "Never refund more than ₹10,000" in the system prompt.
- A tool wrapper that asks for confirmation before a payment.
- A reviewer subagent that double-checks risky actions.

In a self-improving agent, each of these is a **candidate edit**. Even the paper's own safeguard, the critic that
screens out bad changes, is a model step inside the improvement loop.

The paper does not claim its system would remove a refund limit; its subject is generalization, not money. But the
general lesson is hard to ignore: **an optimizer pushes on whatever stands between it and a better score.** It does not
need to be malicious, only good at its job.

## Better at the task is not allowed to do more

Two things get tangled:

- **Capability:** how well the agent does its job. This should improve.
- **Authority:** what the agent is allowed to do: which actions, up to what amount, with whose approval. This should
  not change because the agent got better at its job.

A junior employee who becomes twice as productive does not get to raise their own spending limit. Their manager does,
through a process, with a record. Agents deserve at least that standard. **Limits must sit outside anything the agent
can rewrite.**

## What "outside" means in Parmana

- **The policy lives outside the agent.** Every action is checked against an approved policy the agent does not hold.
- **Changes need two people.** Policies, grants, connectors, business signal sources and approver keys change only
  through maker checker: one identity proposes, a different one approves with a signed step up authorization. A
  non-human key is refused on these routes.
- **Limits are grants, not prompts.** For example, "this agent may refund up to ₹10,000 per request until 31 December"
  is a grant checked on every request, not a sentence the next harness revision might reword.
- **Facts come from the owning system.** An optimized agent might learn that saying "eligible" gets refunds approved, so
  eligibility can be asked of the order system instead.
- **Every decision is signed** with the policy version and the facts it rested on, so behavior can be compared before
  and after an agent's harness changes.

The agent can rewrite its prompts a thousand times. None of those rewrites reach the policy, the grants or the approval
keys.

## What this does not do

Parmana does not make an agent's improvements safe or correct; it bounds what the agent may do while it improves. It
does not inspect or approve harness changes. And the bound is only as strong as the path: if the agent holds payment
credentials of its own, outside the gateway, no authorization layer can stop it.

## The question to ask

Before you let an agent improve itself, ask: **if this agent rewrote everything it is allowed to rewrite, what would
still stop it?** If the answer is its prompt, or a check inside its own loop, that is capability dressed up as control.
